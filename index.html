Before attending

Questions about what lawyers may ask during cross-examination

South Australian Evidence Act 1929, section 34L · plain-English draft

Legal review required before publication. This is a general summary, not legal advice. The exact rules and how they apply depend on the current Act, the question, the evidence, and the case. Ask a qualified South Australian lawyer or witness-support service.
Can I be asked about my sexual history?

Usually, questions about sexual reputation are not allowed. Questions about sexual activity with other people are generally restricted and may only be allowed if the judge gives permission under the law.

The judge must consider your dignity and wellbeing. The question must meet the legal test, including being highly relevant and necessary in the interests of justice. This is a summary only; check the current section 34L wording and get legal advice about your case.

Can the defence lawyer ask about sexual activity with another person?

Usually this evidence is restricted. It is not automatically allowed because a lawyer asks about it. The court decides whether the statutory requirements are met and whether the evidence may be admitted. The exact procedure should be confirmed with your lawyer or witness-support contact.

Can a lawyer ask questions about the allegation and my evidence?

Questions may be asked to test evidence relevant to the case, but the court controls the process and applies rules about admissibility and questioning. A question being asked does not mean the court has accepted it as true or relevant. What may be asked depends on the case and the judge's rulings.

Who decides whether a restricted question can be asked?

The judge or magistrate applies the law to the particular case. If you are concerned about a proposed question, ask your lawyer, prosecutor, or witness-support contact how to raise it before the hearing where possible.

What can I do if a question upsets or confuses me in court?

If you are giving evidence, you can say if you do not understand a question or need it repeated. Ask your legal or witness-support contact beforehand how to request a pause or raise a concern in that courtroom. Do not assume a later complaint will change what happens during a hearing.

Primary source: current authorised Evidence Act 1929 (SA). Read section 34L in the current authorised version; amendments may affect how it operates.

Domestic abuse and Intervention Order pathways (SA)

Some people use “DAIO” informally. SA Courts (Courts Administration Authority) call these Intervention Orders.

Safety first: If you are in immediate danger or feel unsafe, call 000 or go to your nearest police station. This information is general and does not establish whether an order is available or suitable in your circumstances.
What is an Intervention Order?

In South Australia, an Intervention Order is a court order that can place conditions on a person's behaviour to protect another person from abuse. The court decides what conditions, if any, are appropriate. An order is not a finding of guilt in a sexual-offence case and does not replace any criminal case, bail condition, or safety planning.

What pathways are there?

Police-issued pathway

Police may issue an Intervention Order in circumstances allowed by law. Police can explain what they can do and what happens next. The order may then be dealt with through the court process; check the current official guidance for details.

SA Courts (Courts Administration Authority): police-issued Intervention Orders

Applying to the court

A person may be able to apply to the court for an Intervention Order. The Courts Administration Authority explains the application steps, forms, and process. Ask the court or a lawyer about eligibility, safety arrangements, fees (if any), and what evidence or information is needed.

SA Courts (Courts Administration Authority): court application pathway

Does reporting sexual assault automatically give me an Intervention Order?

No. Reporting a sexual offence does not by itself mean an Intervention Order will be made. The criminal investigation and an Intervention Order process are different. Ask police, a lawyer, or a specialist support service about options for your situation.

What can an order say?

Conditions depend on the circumstances and what the court orders. Do not assume an order includes a particular restriction. Read the actual order carefully and ask a lawyer, police, or court support person to explain anything unclear.

What if the order is breached?

If you believe an order has been breached, contact police. If you are in immediate danger, call 000. Keep a record of what happened only if it is safe to do so; do not put yourself at risk to collect evidence.

Can I change or end an order?

Changing, extending, or ending an order involves formal steps and depends on the order and circumstances. Get advice from a lawyer or follow current court guidance. Do not rely on an informal agreement to change the order.

The term “DAIO” may be used informally, but South Australian court information uses “Intervention Order,” “police-issued Intervention Order,” and “court application.” Confirm current terminology and requirements with the official court source.

Appointments and actions

Know My Rights

General information about South Australian processes. What applies depends on your circumstances; ask a qualified lawyer or relevant agency for advice about your situation.

These are practical prompts, not a complete statement of the law. The Victims of Crime Charter describes how victims should be treated and informed. Some information may be restricted by privacy, investigation, or court requirements.

Rights and information to ask about

You can ask the relevant agency how these rights and services apply to you.

Right to information

You can ask police, the prosecutor, or Victims of Crime SA what information can be provided about the process, decisions, and available services. What can be shared may be limited while an investigation or court matter is active.

Right to safety

Tell police, the prosecutor, or a support worker about safety concerns, intimidation, or changes in circumstances as soon as you can. Ask what safety planning, court arrangements, or protective options may be available; they are not automatic and depend on the situation. If you are in immediate danger, call 000.

Right to support services

You can ask about victim support, counselling, practical court support, interpreters, and accessibility arrangements. Services, eligibility, and availability vary. Victims of Crime SA and the Sexual Violence Legal Service can explain relevant services.

Right to make a Victim Impact Statement

If the accused is found guilty, you may be able to provide a Victim Impact Statement for the court to consider at sentencing. Ask the prosecutor or witness-support service whether it is available in your case, how to prepare one, and when it is needed. The court decides what it can consider.

Right to ask questions

You can ask your police contact, prosecutor, lawyer, or support worker to explain processes and decisions in plain language. Some details cannot be disclosed, and asking a question does not determine what decision will be made.

Right to update contact details

Tell the relevant police officer, prosecutor, and any victim-support or notification service if your phone number, email, or address changes. Ask each service to confirm that its records have been updated; one update may not change another agency's records.

Right to complain

You can ask an organisation how to raise a concern about its service or conduct. Complaint bodies have different roles and limits; a complaint is not an appeal and may not change a prosecution or court decision. See the South Australian complaint pathways for more detail.

What happens when things go wrong?

Ask who made a decision, what can be explained, and what options may remain.

No charge laid

An investigation may not lead to a charge. Different legal and evidential factors can be involved. Ask the investigating agency who made the decision, what information can be shared, whether the investigation remains open, and how to provide relevant new information. Do not assume a charge will be laid or that a particular review process is available.

If the Director of Public Prosecutions decides not to proceed

Ask the DPP or your prosecutor to explain what can be shared about the decision and whether any request for review or reconsideration is available. There is no guarantee that a decision can be reviewed or changed. If you have new information, ask the investigating officer or prosecutor how to provide it and whether it can be assessed.

Why was the matter discontinued?
  • Can someone explain why the case will not continue?
  • Who made the decision?
  • What factors were considered?
  • Was the decision based on evidence, legal issues, public interest considerations, or something else?
Can I receive information, an explanation, or documents?
  • Can I receive information about the decision or request an explanation?
  • Who should I contact?
  • Can I obtain documents relating to the decision?
  • Are there limits on what can be disclosed?
  • Who can explain the decision to me?
Can the decision be reviewed, reconsidered, or affected by new evidence?
  • Do I have a right to seek a review? Is there a process for having the decision reconsidered?
  • Can new evidence or other information be submitted, and who should I contact?
  • What happens if further information becomes available later? Will it be assessed?

Ask the DPP or investigating agency whether a process applies to your circumstances. This app cannot determine eligibility for a review or predict its outcome.

What happens to my rights, support, and safety?
  • What happens to my rights as a victim now?
  • Can I still access support services, Victims of Crime compensation, or provide information about the impact of the offence?
  • What support remains available to me?
  • Does the decision affect any Intervention Order? Are bail conditions still in force?
  • What options exist if I have ongoing safety concerns?

Ask police or a lawyer to confirm the current status of any order or condition. Do not assume that a prosecution decision changes an order or safety arrangement.

What if I disagree with the decision or have a complaint?
  • Can I make a complaint about how I was treated?
  • What organisations can provide independent advice?
  • What if new evidence is found or additional information becomes available after the case is discontinued?

A complaint about service or conduct is different from asking about a prosecution decision. See South Australian complaint pathways or ask an independent lawyer about options.

When charges are withdrawn or the matter is discontinued

Ask the prosecutor what happened to the charge, whether the case is finished, and whether any further court date or action is expected. Reasons and available information vary. Ask the prosecutor or lawyer to explain any effect on safety arrangements, orders, or support.

Acquittal (a not guilty verdict)

A not guilty verdict means the prosecution did not prove the charge to the required legal standard. It does not establish that you were not believed. Ask the prosecutor to explain the outcome and any next steps; any appeal rights, grounds, and strict time limits depend on the law and circumstances. Support remains available.

Long delays

One common justice barrier is silence.

Long delays can occur for many reasons. If you are concerned, consider contacting:

  • Investigating officer
  • Prosecutor
  • Victims of Crime SA
  • Your lawyer

Ask who can provide an update and when you should follow up. Contacting an agency does not guarantee a particular date or outcome.

What happened?

Every situation is different. The prosecution may have ended because:

  • No charges were laid.
  • Charges were withdrawn.
  • The Director of Public Prosecutions decided not to proceed.
  • The matter was discontinued.
  • A court found the accused not guilty.
  • Another legal issue prevented the case from continuing.

Questions you may wish to ask

You may wish to ask the relevant agency, lawyer, prosecutor, police officer, or support worker:

  • Can the decision be explained to me?
  • What factors led to the decision?
  • Is there any further information available?
  • If new evidence becomes available, who should I contact?
  • What support services remain available to me?
  • Are there any other options I should consider?

Record any answers in your Questions & Notes section.

Other options that may still be available

These pathways are separate from a criminal prosecution and may not apply to everyone.

Victims of Crime compensation

In some circumstances, financial assistance or compensation pathways may remain available even when a criminal prosecution does not proceed. Ask:

  • Am I eligible?
  • Are time limits involved?
  • What documents should I keep?
  • Who can assist me with an application?

Open the Victims of Crime Compensation section and confirm current requirements with Victims of Crime SA or a qualified adviser.

Intervention Orders and safety options

If you continue to have safety concerns, ask about Intervention Orders, safety planning, victim support services, or police assistance. A criminal conviction is not the only pathway through which safety concerns may be addressed. Options depend on your circumstances; seek advice from police, a lawyer, or a specialist support service.

Civil legal options

Some people speak with a lawyer about possible civil legal action. Ask whether any civil claims are available, what evidence would be required, whether time limits apply, what costs might be involved, and what outcomes a civil court can provide. A lawyer can explain whether any civil option may apply to your circumstances.

Professional conduct complaints

Where appropriate, concerns may be raised through professional registration boards, regulatory bodies, conduct commissioners, or an organisation's internal complaint pathway. Each organisation has different processes and powers. See the complaint information in the Court Process Guide.

Justice Navigator Support

A decision not to proceed can leave people feeling unsure about what options remain. The Sexual Violence Legal Service may be able to help you understand:

  • the decision that was made
  • available complaint pathways
  • Victims of Crime compensation
  • Intervention Orders
  • civil legal options
  • available support services

Support is guided by your own goals and circumstances.

Looking after yourself

The end of a criminal case can bring many different reactions. Some people feel relief; others feel disappointed, angry, confused, frustrated, or exhausted. You may feel a combination of these feelings.

Support remains available even when legal proceedings have ended. Consider contacting your support worker, a trusted support person, victim-support services, counselling services, or legal assistance services.

A criminal prosecution is only one part of a person's journey. You can still seek support, ask questions, access information, make informed decisions about your future, and discuss available options with qualified professionals.

This page provides general information only and is not legal advice. If you are unsure about your circumstances, seek advice from a qualified lawyer or relevant support service.

    Therapy, support, and remembering

    Receiving therapy and seeking justice should not feel like an either/or choice. Seeking support for your wellbeing is important.

    Legal proceedings can sometimes raise questions about access to or use of counselling records. Whether that is relevant depends on the circumstances and applicable law; it does not mean that records will automatically be requested or used. If you have concerns about privacy or how treatment may interact with your matter, ask your lawyer or support person for advice specific to your circumstances. Do not delay seeking support solely because you are involved in a legal process.

    I remembered something after making my statement. Is that unusual?

    No. Trauma can affect memory and recall. Some people remember additional details over time.

    If you remember something important after providing a statement, discuss it with your lawyer, prosecutor, investigating officer, or support worker as soon as possible.

    Recording a recollection in this journal does not notify authorities.

    Does receiving counselling affect my rights?

    Many people receive counselling or trauma-focused therapy while involved in criminal justice processes. Receiving counselling does not automatically mean you lose your rights or should stop seeking support.

    If you have concerns about how treatment or related records may interact with your matter, discuss them with a qualified lawyer or support service.

    Grounding exercise

    Take a moment to notice what is around you. Stop whenever you wish.

    1. Name five things you can see.
    2. Notice four things you can feel beneath your hands or feet.
    3. Listen for three sounds around you.
    4. Notice two scents, or take two slow comfortable breaths.
    5. Name one thing you need right now.

    Privacy, security, and data controls

    This standalone HTML app uses this browser's local storage. It does not provide user accounts, strong authentication, encryption at rest, secure file storage, or access controls. Anyone who can use this browser profile may see saved information. Do not use this prototype for highly sensitive records on a shared device.

    You control retention: export a backup if needed, then use “Delete all app data” below to remove saved app information from this browser. Backups are unencrypted. No email or calendar account is connected to this app. Message drafts open in your device's messaging app, and calendar events are downloaded as files for you to review and import yourself.

    Before publication: This prototype does not satisfy production security requirements for sensitive health, contact, or case information. Add a security-reviewed backend, authentication, encryption, access controls, secure file storage, retention policy, and qualified South Australian legal review before public deployment.

    Privacy and support

    Your safety and control over information come first.

    Saved information is stored in this browser's local storage and is not encrypted by this app. This includes message activity such as the recipient and time a draft was prepared, but not the message text. When you prepare a message, its draft is handed to your device's messaging app; you choose whether to send it, and this app cannot confirm delivery. Copies may remain in that app, your device, backups, or service-provider records. Anyone with access to this device or browser profile may be able to view local records. Avoid shared devices, use a device lock, and export a backup before clearing browser data. Exported files are unencrypted; store them somewhere secure.

    Immediate danger? Contact your local emergency services. For confidential support, contact a trusted local sexual assault service or victim support organisation.
